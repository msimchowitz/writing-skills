# Sponsor-type guidance

Classify the primary funder before choosing the proposal's theory of value.
Preserve the same direct, technical voice across both categories.

## For-profit or company-sponsored proposals

Center the company as a consequential user, partner, or deployment setting.
Assume the sponsor cares about research quality and about strategic,
operational, or economic value.

Treat the company's profit motive as a reason to explain economic or strategic
value, risk reduction, and paths to adoption. Do not reduce a research proposal
to a near-term revenue pitch.

### Motivation

- State the core problem through the company's real systems, users, workflows,
  constraints, or risks.
- Explain the cost of the problem in terms such as reliability, latency,
  safety, throughput, compute, labor, risk, adoption, or competitive position.
- Use company-specific examples when the supplied material supports them.
- Show why the problem becomes harder at the company's scale.

### Fit and relationships

- Emphasize relevant existing connections: prior awards, joint work, company
  collaborators, shared technical interests, access to realistic problems, or
  earlier successful engagements.
- Explain how each connection lowers execution risk or improves the work. Do not
  list names without a role in the story.
- Identify credible paths from research to testing, integration, adoption, or
  future collaboration.
- Name the company where specificity matters, but do not repeat its name as a
  substitute for substance.

### Track record and impact

- Foreground past achievements that show technical authority and practical
  execution.
- Connect prior deployment, industry adoption, or sponsor-relevant work to the
  new agenda.
- Define deliverables the sponsor can evaluate: prototypes, benchmarks, code,
  studies, metrics, papers, or demonstrations.
- Explain direct sponsor value and broader scientific value without turning the
  proposal into sales copy.

## Nonprofit or philanthropic proposals

Center the funder's vision and the scientific or societal change it seeks.
Assume the funder cares about mission progress, neglected problems, durable
knowledge, public value, and the field created by successful work.

### Motivation

- Lead with the scientific or societal problem, who or what it affects, and why
  it remains unresolved.
- Explain why the problem matters under the funder's vision, not how the work
  benefits the funding organization itself.
- Show why the problem is timely, neglected, under-resourced, or newly
  tractable.
- Describe the field-level consequence of success: new understanding, safer
  systems, shared infrastructure, evidence, methods, talent, or coordination.

### Fit and relationships

- Establish fit through the program's stated goals, worldview, and theory of
  change.
- Do not emphasize existing personal or institutional connections to the
  organization.
- Mention an existing relationship only when disclosure is required or the
  relationship supplies a concrete resource essential to execution. Do not use
  access as evidence of merit.
- Do not frame impact as direct value, prestige, or advantage for the funder.
- Avoid generic praise and close paraphrases of the funder's mission statement.

### Track record and impact

- Use prior achievements to establish that the team can attack the problem at
  the required depth and scale.
- Connect the team's trajectory to the funder's vision without implying that
  familiarity with the organization gives the application special standing.
- Emphasize knowledge, public goods, community capacity, open resources,
  standards, or long-run scientific and societal outcomes when accurate.
- Explain why this team, this problem, and this moment can produce unusual
  progress.

## Hybrid and unclear cases

When a corporate foundation, prize, or public-private program mixes incentives:

1. Read the legal sponsor type, program language, scoring rubric, and past award
   descriptions.
2. Identify whether reviewers are buying strategic value, advancing a mission,
   or doing both.
3. Choose one primary theory of value and keep the secondary case brief.
4. Ask the user when the program materials do not make the priority clear.

Do not blend both stories indiscriminately. A proposal that promises corporate
return and philanthropic impact in every paragraph usually makes neither case
well.
