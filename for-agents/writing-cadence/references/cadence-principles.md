# Cadence principles

Cadence is the pattern of emphasis a reader experiences across clauses,
sentences, and paragraphs. It depends on length, but sentence length alone is
a weak proxy. Syntax, information order, punctuation, and repetition determine
where the prose accelerates or asks the reader to pause.

## Variation serves emphasis

Purdue OWL recommends varying sentence length and structure because repeated
patterns become monotonous. Longer sentences can hold related information;
shorter sentences can emphasize an important point. This does not imply a
fixed alternation. A paragraph should vary when its ideas have different
weights and relationships.

For short, choppy prose, Purdue recommends coordination or subordination.
Coordination joins claims of comparable weight. Subordination marks one clause
as a cause, condition, concession, or other modifier of the main claim. The
choice should therefore follow the relation between ideas rather than the
desire to make a sentence longer.

Changing an opening also changes emphasis. Do not front a prepositional phrase
or dependent clause only to avoid starting another sentence with "The" or
"We." Make the change when the new opening supplies a condition, links to the
previous sentence, or places the intended subject in focus.

## Readers use structural positions

George Gopen and Judith Swan argue that readers interpret scientific prose
through recurring structural expectations. Four principles are especially
useful for cadence:

1. Keep a grammatical subject close to its verb.
2. Use the opening, or topic position, to link backward with familiar context.
3. Put important new information near syntactic closure, where readers
   naturally assign stress.
4. Express the sentence's main action in its verb.

These are interpretive principles, not mechanical rules. A skilled writer may
delay a verb or move new information forward for a specific effect. The
surrounding prose must make that effect legible.

## Paragraph-level diagnostics

Cadence becomes mechanical when several sentences repeat any combination of:

- the same approximate length;
- the same opening word or phrase;
- the same clause count;
- the same subject-verb-complement skeleton;
- the same contrast formula;
- the same inventory structure; or
- the same emphatic ending.

A paragraph break does not reset the pattern. Read across the break and across
section openings. Display text can contribute to staccato rhythm when a short
heading is followed by a one-line paragraph and another heading.

## Contrast formulas

Research prose needs contrast, but a sequence of reusable difference frames
sounds generated and can blur distinct scientific relations. Search for:

- "not X but Y";
- "not only X but also Y";
- "X, not Y";
- "treats X as Y, not Z";
- "treats X like Y rather than Z";
- "rather than merely X";
- "unlike X, Y"; and
- repeated openings with "while," "whereas," or "in contrast."

Retain the negation when a negative result or claim boundary matters. To vary
the syntax without weakening the distinction, state the supported positive
claim first, name the comparator in a separate clause, or let the evidence
establish the contrast.

## Spoken review

Read the passage at speaking pace after revision. A useful spoken audit asks:

- Does one sentence require several unrelated breaths?
- Do several sentences stop at the same interval?
- Does the voice rise repeatedly for manufactured punchlines?
- Does routine setup receive stronger closure than the result?
- Can the reader hear which claim governs the paragraph?

The final revision should sound purposeful rather than conspicuously varied.
