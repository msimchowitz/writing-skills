# Writing Cadence

Shape rhythm around meaning and emphasis. Preserve every supported claim,
qualification, citation, and recognizable voice signal. Sentence variety is a
result of clear relationships, not a target sequence of short and long lines.

Read [cadence-principles.md](references/cadence-principles.md) when the task
requires a full cadence edit or an explanation of the underlying principles.

## Diagnose The Passage

Read at paragraph scale before changing individual sentences. Mark:

- runs of three or more sentences of roughly ten words or fewer;
- several neighboring sentences with similar lengths;
- repeated openings, especially "This," "The," "It," "We," or the same
  dependent-clause frame;
- repeated subject-verb-complement skeletons;
- long sentences that contain more than one main inference;
- long gaps between a grammatical subject and its verb;
- endings that place routine setup after the important result; and
- paragraphs whose final sentence merely restates the topic, shifts to
  housekeeping, or adds a vague conclusion; and
- paragraph breaks that conceal the same rhythm on both sides.

Treat these as diagnostics. A run may be deliberate, and a technical passage
may need repeated syntax for a fixed taxonomy or proof. Compare the pattern
with the writer's sample and the genre before revising it.

## Scan Repeated Contrast Frames

Mark every contrast built from a reusable difference template, including:

- "not X but Y" and "not only X but also Y";
- "This is not X. It is Y.";
- "X, not Y" used as a sentence-ending correction;
- "treats X as Y, not Z";
- "treats X like Y rather than Z";
- "rather than merely X, Y";
- repeated "unlike X, Y" openings; and
- several neighboring sentences built around "while," "whereas," or "in
  contrast."

Keep a contrast when the distinction limits a scientific claim or separates
two mechanisms. Revise repeated templates by stating the positive claim,
naming the governing comparison once, or distributing the distinction across
different sentence structures. Do not erase a meaningful negative result.

## Rebuild Rhythm From Relationships

1. Identify the point that deserves emphasis in each sentence and paragraph.
2. Link backward near the opening with the subject, condition, or established
   context that the reader already knows.
3. Keep the subject near its verb unless an intervening phrase is truly
   secondary.
4. Place the important new result near syntactic closure when that ordering
   matches the logic.
5. Join short neighboring sentences when one supplies the other's cause,
   condition, contrast, or consequence.
6. Split a long sentence when it makes two independent claims, changes
   subjects repeatedly, or postpones the governing verb.
7. Vary openings only when the new opening changes emphasis or improves the
   link to the previous sentence.
8. Reserve a very short sentence for a conclusion that merits the pause.

Do not alternate lengths mechanically, add fragments for texture, or move a
modifier merely to avoid repetition. These changes produce visible variety
without improving the reading experience.

## Let Paragraphs End Where The Reasoning Arrives

Identify the paragraph's intellectual destination before revising its last
sentence. A supported consequence, a condition that limits the claim, an
unresolved tension, or a concrete fact can provide natural closure. A
transition may also end quietly when the next paragraph supplies the
consequence.

Do not append a summary, slogan, generic implication, or sentence about the
document merely because the paragraph seems to need a conclusion. If the
penultimate sentence already completes the thought, cut the weaker ending. If
the important point appears earlier, reorder the paragraph instead of writing
a more emphatic final line. Audit abstracts, section openings, and section
closings separately because readers assign those positions additional weight.

## Read At Speaking Pace

Read the revision aloud or at a deliberate speaking pace. Check where a
reader naturally pauses, which words receive stress, and whether syntactic
closure lands on the intended information. Rework any passage that:

- requires the same breath pattern several times in a row;
- gives equal emphasis to claims of unequal importance;
- makes the reader wait too long for the main verb;
- ends several sentences with the same grammatical shape; or
- repeatedly closes paragraphs with a miniature summary or slogan; or
- sounds varied on a sentence-length count but monotonous aloud.

Compare the revision with the source once more. Restore any technical
distinction, uncertainty, or personal cadence lost during restructuring.
