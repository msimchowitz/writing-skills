\section{A common standard across the writing skills}
\label{sec:revision}

The skills designed to humanize agent writing address recurring agent defaults
from different directions, but their diagnoses converge. None defines human
prose as a collection of preferred words. Together, they describe writing in
which the reader, evidence, semantic relation, and rhythm constrain one
another.

\subsection{Humanizer identifies reusable surface defaults}

Humanizer provides the broadest catalog. It detects inflated significance,
promotional language, vague attribution, chat residue, repeated contrast
frames, loose catalogs, mismatched relations, and mechanical endings. The
patterns differ, but many have the same effect: a familiar form replaces a
decision specific to the passage.

The catalog is diagnostic rather than forensic. Human writers can produce any
of these patterns, and agents can avoid them. Their value lies in the reading
effects they expose. Some hide agency or inflate an evaluation; others flatten
priority or detach rhythm from meaning.

\subsection{Usage, cadence, and paragraph structure}

Better-usage operates inside the sentence. It tests whether the subject can
perform its verb, whether the object fits that verb, and whether the sentence
has omitted an intermediate such as a claim's extent or a theorem's
consequences. This is the most direct answer to semantic sloppiness.

Writing-cadence examines rhythm across a passage. At that scale, staccato runs,
uniform lengths, repeated openings, and weak endings become visible. Its
standard is purposeful emphasis: writers join or divide sentences so the prose
accelerates and pauses with the argument.

The non-autoregressive writing pass begins only after the draft exists as a
whole. It rereads the title, subtitle, headings, and paragraph openings against
the finished argument, then places each paragraph's last sentence beside the
next opening. This wider view catches a heading or topic sentence that promises
a weaker claim than the passage develops, along with locally fluent
transitions that misstate the relation between paragraphs.

\subsection{The reader's problem in academic prose and abstracts}

Academic-voice describes a writer who is careful and precise, with a strong
interest in being understood. It rejects corporate and social-media registers,
whose recognizable postures often replace exact claims. Paper-writing adds
discipline about claims and evidence; its sentence guidance keeps the main
subject and verb visible and avoids packing several inferences into one line.

Abstract-writing exposes the difference between coverage and understanding.
An agent abstract often summarizes sections or inventories contributions. A
strong human abstract gives the reader enough to grasp the problem, central
answer, decisive evidence, and conditions that determine the claim's extent.
Its ending completes the inference rather than announcing future work or
repeating the contribution.

\subsection{General-writing preserves the writer during revision}

General-writing combines broad cleanup with comparison against the source.
That comparison matters because fluent revision can erase the very features
that made the draft human: a specific fact, calibrated uncertainty, an abrupt
but purposeful transition, or an unusual cadence the writer can defend.
Humanization succeeds only when the writer remains recognizable.

Good human writing is distinguished by choices that follow from the argument
and the needs of a particular reader; neither messiness nor polish establishes
that standard. A reader can tell why a fact appears, why one sentence is long
and another short, why a qualification interrupts, and why the paragraph ends
where it does. Agent prose feels choppy or sloppy when it falls back on reusable
forms without enough information about the argument or reader.
