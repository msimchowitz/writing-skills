\section{Possible causes of recurring agent-like patterns}
\label{sec:hypotheses}

Output patterns do not identify their own causes. I treat the explanations
below as hypotheses about different stages of generation, not as settled
accounts of why a model writes as it does. A deployed language agent begins
with pretraining, may undergo several forms of post-training, and then
generates under system instructions and a decoding procedure; retrieval or
tool use can also change the evidence available at generation time. Several of
these mechanisms may operate together.

\subsection{Local prediction may weaken larger structures}

During pretraining, a language model learns to predict each next token from the
available preceding context. Later training changes the model's behavior, but
familiar phrases and locally smooth word sequences may remain attractive even
when the sentence or paragraph requires a less common construction.

Local plausibility does not guarantee that a relation will remain clear across
the full sentence. This pressure could produce stock transitions and repeated
rhetorical frames; a penalty against lexical repetition could produce the
opposite surface pattern, synonym cycling. The outputs look different, but in
both cases a locally reasonable choice weakens the larger structure.

If local prediction competes with paragraph structure, an explicit paragraph
plan should reduce relation-level errors without changing the content to be
expressed. A matched evaluation could compare outputs with and without that
plan, measuring factual preservation and the clarity of the resulting
relations. Such a result would test a behavioral consequence of the hypothesis,
not identify the training stage that caused it.

\subsection{An underspecified audience may produce generic prose}

A prompt can specify a task without giving the model a stable representation
of the person who will use the answer. Generic helpfulness then fills the gap:
the response explains broadly, announces transitions, and labels ideas
``important'' without selecting the consequence that matters in context.

Readerless corporate shorthand fits this account. ``Give each section one
job'' transfers across genres because it presupposes almost nothing.
``Each section should help a reviewer answer one question'' depends on a
reader with a particular decision and may therefore produce a different
selection of detail.

Testing this account requires more than tone labels. A matched comparison
could hold subject and length constant while varying the reader's prior
knowledge and pending decision. Members of the named audience could then
evaluate whether each version supports the stated use.

\subsection{Mixed training data may favor a middle register}

Training corpora span genres, institutions, eras, and levels of editorial
quality. Without a strong voice cue, a model may settle on language acceptable
across much of that mixture. Common evaluative words and regular paragraph
shapes are relatively portable, whereas unusual specifics create greater risk.

Code and API documentation may exert a more particular influence, as may issue
trackers and technical manuals. These genres rely on modular headings and
explicit labels; they also make edge cases and procedures easy to scan.
Transferred into an essay, the same conventions can yield tiny sections and
prose that resembles an interface. This is a conjecture about cross-genre
transfer, not a claim that technical documentation is poorly written.

Style conditioning offers one test. Output with no sample could be compared
with output conditioned on one sample and then on several samples from the
same genre. Differences among models with documented training emphases would
provide another, less controlled comparison.

\subsection{Post-training may favor answers that look complete}

Instruction tuning and preference optimization may favor features that make an
answer easy to evaluate as helpful. In a chat interface, headings, long lists,
recaps, balanced caveats, and offers to continue can look thorough even when
they weaken a finished document. The same incentives may reward automatic
agreement:
``Great question'' and ``You're absolutely right'' preserve rapport without
testing the underlying claim.

Stacked hedges can also present the appearance of caution while leaving the
actual uncertainty unspecified. The cleanest comparison would pair a base
model with an instruction-tuned model from the same family on a matched
continuation task. A second factor, chat reply versus finished document, would
test whether the model can condition those habits on genre.

\subsection{Answer pressure may increase unsupported completion}

Some prompts demand an answer that the supplied evidence cannot support.
Vague attribution and plausible gap-filling allow generation to continue, and
the absence of evidence may itself become material for a familiar story. A
private person with little public documentation, for example, can acquire an
invented preference for privacy.

Retrieval and explicit permission to answer ``unknown'' provide direct
interventions on this hypothesis. An experiment could vary both, then check
every factual claim against the evidence supplied in that condition. Fluency
would not compensate for an unsupported claim.

These explanations can reinforce one another. A familiar phrase supplied by
local prediction may survive because mixed training data make it broadly
acceptable. Post-training can preserve it when it resembles a complete answer,
and an underspecified reader gives the model no reason to remove irrelevant
material. Experiments that vary one factor at a time would clarify which
mechanism changes each language outcome.
