\section{Semantic precision}
\label{sec:directness}

Agents can produce grammatically correct sentences while remaining imprecise
about semantic relations. They may assign an abstract noun an action it cannot
perform, pair a verb with the wrong object, or omit an intermediate needed to
state the intended relation. Human prose becomes more exact when the writer
notices these mismatches and names the link that the sentence requires.

\subsection{The fit between subjects and verbs}

\agentdraft

A careful reader should understand the evidence behind the claim and where the
claim stops.

\humanrevision

A careful reader should understand the evidence supporting the claim and the
conditions that limit its extent.

The first sentence contains two loose relations. Evidence may support or
contradict a claim, or remain consistent with it. The claim itself does not
travel toward a stopping point. Its validity depends on stated conditions, and
its implications need not extend beyond the regime in which it applies.

Changing one verb is not always enough. ``The assumptions limit the claim'' is
still imprecise because it does not identify what becomes narrower. The
assumptions may limit the claim's extent, or the claim may hold only under
those assumptions. The missing noun carries part of the meaning.

\subsection{Human writers make agency recoverable}

Agent prose frequently relies on passive constructions, subjectless fragments,
or document personification because each lets the writer continue without
identifying who acts. ``The results are preserved automatically'' may be adequate
when the actor is irrelevant. When responsibility matters, ``the system
preserves the results'' gives the reader information the passive version
withholds.

Documents can stand for their authors in familiar scholarly shorthand: a paper
may argue, report, or show. The shorthand becomes sloppy when it crosses into
an action that belongs to a person or analysis. A section can state an answer,
but it does not know one. Researchers ask a question; an experimental design
tests it.

\subsection{Direct verbs expose the mechanism}

Abstract wrappers such as ``the mechanism behind'' or ``the role of'' name the
existence of a relation without stating it. Replacing one of these wrappers
with a direct verb requires the writer to identify the relation. One variable
may cause, constrain, predict, or merely correlate with another. Those
alternatives are not stylistic variants: they make different claims about the
evidence.

Simple verbs are often more exact than inflated ones: ``is'' and ``has'' state
their relations plainly, while ``can process'' is clearer than ``has the
ability to process.'' Phrases such as ``serves as'' or ``boasts'' add weight
without adding specificity. A stronger verb helps only when the evidence
supports a more specific action.

\subsection{Attribution and uncertainty remain visible}

Human research prose distinguishes direct evidence from interpretation. A
measurement may show a difference, several experiments may support an
explanation, and an observed association may only suggest a mechanism.
Changing ``suggests'' to ``demonstrates'' does more than tighten the sentence;
it strengthens the claim.

Agent prose can also obscure a claim's source or leave the uncertainty itself
unspecified. Vague phrases such as ``experts argue'' borrow authority from
unnamed sources, while stacked hedges such as ``could potentially possibly''
accumulate caution without locating the uncertainty. Precise prose names the
source when one exists and chooses the single evidential verb or condition
that matches what is known.
