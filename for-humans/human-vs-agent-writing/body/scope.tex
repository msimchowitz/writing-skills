\section{Situated judgment}
\label{sec:scope}

The strongest difference between good human prose and common agent prose lies
in how deliberately a human writer organizes the argument as a whole and
prioritizes its essential points. These choices determine which fact deserves
space, which comparison matters, how much confidence the evidence permits,
and where the reasoning is complete. A thoughtful human writer makes them for
a particular reader. An agent often has to infer the same priorities from a
prompt that leaves the reader and purpose only partly specified.

This is a comparison of writing defaults, not a claim that every person writes
well or every agent writes badly. People produce vague, mechanical prose, and
agents can produce precise, situated prose when the task supplies enough
context and the revision process is strong. The useful comparison is between
passages organized by deliberate judgment about one situation and passages
organized by portable patterns that could fit many.

\subsection{A particular reader}

A writer with a particular reader in view estimates what that person already
knows, why they are reading, and which uncertainty will matter to their next
inference. Those estimates govern the selection of detail. A reviewer checking
a result may need the experimental controls early, while a student learning
the mechanism may need conceptual setup before those controls have meaning.

The same judgment governs emphasis. A surprising result receives more space
than routine setup, and a limitation appears as soon as it changes the claim.
An example remains when it reveals something the abstraction misses. Pace
changes with these decisions: conditions may require a longer sentence, while
routine connective material can move quickly. A short sentence can mark a
conclusion.

\subsection{Local fluency}

Agent prose can be excellent at the scale of a phrase or sentence while
remaining weak at the scale of a paragraph. Each sentence sounds plausible,
but the passage may not establish which idea governs the others. Background,
evidence, caveats, examples, and conclusions can receive similar weight
because familiar completion patterns supply the organization that the prompt
did not.

This local fluency explains why agent writing can feel strangely polished and
unfinished at the same time. The document may look orderly in every visible
respect, yet the reader still has to determine which claim matters and why the
listed facts belong together. Its intellectual hierarchy remains unclear.

\subsection{Choppiness and sloppiness}

\emph{Choppiness} concerns movement. A passage feels choppy when its sentences
do not inherit their pace from the relations among ideas. Several clipped
sentences can give routine observations false drama, but a run of
medium-length sentences can be equally mechanical when each follows the same
grammatical path. Sentence length is only the most visible symptom.

\emph{Sloppiness} concerns semantic fit. A grammatical sentence can still
misstate the relation among its parts. Claims do not ``stop,'' evidence does
not necessarily ``explain,'' and a section does not literally ``know'' an
answer. Generic emphasis and vague attribution create a similar looseness:
tone seems to provide support that the sentence never names.

These defects often reinforce each other. When a writer has not identified the
relation among the ideas, the prose tends to list them in separate,
similarly shaped units. Once the relation is precise, the writer can choose
syntax and cadence that express it.
