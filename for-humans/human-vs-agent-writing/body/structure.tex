\section{Visible organization can flatten priority}
\label{sec:structure}

A document can look orderly while making its central claim harder to find.
Agent-like answers can acquire headings and bullets before the dependencies
among their ideas are clear. Once every item receives the same visual weight,
the reader must reconstruct which claims support, qualify, or merely
illustrate the others.

\subsection{Lists clarify coherent sets}

Lists work well when readers need to scan a fixed set or follow a sequence.
Procedures and option sets often meet this condition. Connected claims usually
belong in prose because their relationships matter more than their count.

A five-item threshold is useful only as a diagnostic; it has no mathematical
status. Long lists can contain several unnamed groups, and short lists can
still combine objects that serve unrelated functions. The form helps only when
a governing relation makes the set coherent.

Forced triads create a subtler version of the problem. A sentence may group
three claims merely because the cadence sounds complete, even though the
subject has no three-part structure. False ranges have a similar effect.
``From hiring to reporting'' implies a scale absent from the underlying
topics; ``hiring and reporting'' simply names them.

\subsection{Headings reveal information architecture}

A heading marks a real change in question, object, or level of analysis.
One-line sections and sentences that repeat their headings create the
appearance of navigation without reducing uncertainty. Bold label-and-colon
bullets can make ordinary prose resemble a dashboard for the same reason.

Announcements such as ``Let's dive in'' or ``Here's what you need to know''
delay the content promised by the heading. Diff-anchored documentation
similarly draws attention to the latest edit instead of describing the current
system. Version-scoped documents are exceptions because change is their
subject.

\subsection{Cadence reflects the structure of the argument}

Cadence depends on information order and clause structure as much as sentence
length. Familiar context can appear near the opening, while an important
result receives the stress of syntactic closure. A short sentence can
emphasize a conclusion, but a run of clipped declarations gives every idea the
same dramatic weight. Medium-length sentences can sound just as mechanical
when each one ends with the same grammatical shape.

\agentdraft

Then the new model arrived. No warning. No compromise. The old rules were
gone.

\humanrevision

The new model changed the comparison because it did not share the assumptions
of the earlier systems.

The revision joins the event with its explanation, so the causal relation
determines the rhythm. A repeated pattern such as ``treats X as Y, not Z'' can
be equally mechanical when it appears across neighboring paragraphs.
Scientific distinctions often require contrast, especially when the contrast
prevents an overclaim, but no single template fits all of them.

Slogan formulas manufacture a different kind of stress. ``Symmetry is the
language of trust'' sounds complete while leaving the relation unspecified. A
claim about symmetric layouts making controls easier to predict identifies an
effect that evidence could support or refute.

\subsection{Typography cannot establish provenance}

Mechanical boldface, title case in every heading, decorative emoji, and
inline-header lists can accompany agent-like prose. They become editing
problems when they obscure hierarchy or conflict with the publication, not
because any one of them reveals an author.

Punctuation also reflects the venue's conventions and the writer's established
preferences. Humanizer removes dashes because its catalog treats them as a
common default rhythm device; the broader general-writing standard permits an
occasional dash when it matches the author's sample and improves the sentence.
Curly quotation marks are likewise a typographic convention. Neither mark has
much forensic value by itself.

\subsection{Stable names reduce memory demands}

Synonym cycling can make one object appear to change. A paragraph that rotates
among ``the model,'' ``the system,'' ``the framework,'' and ``the approach''
requires the reader to test whether each phrase has a new referent. Repeating
the canonical term is quieter and more precise; variation becomes useful only
when it expresses a real distinction.
