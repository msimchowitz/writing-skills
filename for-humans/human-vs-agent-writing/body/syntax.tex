\section{Cadence}
\label{sec:syntax}

Cadence is the rhythm produced by the relations among ideas. Writers express
causes and conditions differently from contrasts and consequences. Good human
writing therefore varies as the thought develops. Agent writing often varies
too little or relies on obvious templates for variation.

\subsection{Short sentences do not create emphasis by themselves}

\agentdraft

Then the new model arrived. No warning. No compromise. The old rules were
gone.

\humanrevision

The new model changed the comparison because its design did not rely on the
assumptions built into the earlier systems.

The clipped version gives every phrase dramatic closure, including details
that have not earned it. The revision joins the event to its explanation, so
the causal relation determines the pace. A short sentence would still be
appropriate if the paragraph had reached a conclusion worth isolating.

\subsection{Uniform medium sentences can be equally choppy}

\agentdraft

The study evaluates the controller in simulation. The study measures
robustness under observation noise. The study compares performance across
three tasks. The study reports consistent gains.

\humanrevision

The study evaluates the controller in simulation and measures its robustness
to observation noise across three tasks. It reports consistent gains in each
task.

Nothing in the first passage is fragmentary, yet the repeated subject and
sentence length make the prose advance in separate, equally weighted steps.
The revision combines aspects of the same evaluation, then states the
cross-task result in a separate sentence. Its change in pace follows the
relation among the claims rather than a schedule of long and short sentences.

\subsection{Openings and endings distribute stress}

Readers use the opening of a sentence to connect with established context and
its syntactic closure to identify what deserves emphasis. A subject placed
near its verb is easier to track; an important result placed near the end of
the sentence receives natural stress. These are tendencies rather than a
template, but they explain why rearrangement can improve cadence without
changing sentence length.

Mechanical variation has the opposite effect. Fronting a prepositional phrase
only to avoid another sentence beginning with ``The'' moves emphasis without a
reason. Alternating long and short sentences by count produces variety on the
page while leaving the spoken rhythm artificial.

\subsection{Contrast needs more than one syntax}

Research writing depends on contrast when a result must be interpreted against
an alternative or kept within its proper scope. The agent-like tendency lies
in repeating the same frame after the scientific relation changes:
``not X but Y,'' ``treats X as Y, not Z,'' and ``rather than merely X, Y.''

A human writer can state the supported positive claim first. The comparator
may appear in a separate clause, or the evidence itself may establish the
difference. A causal contrast and a scope limitation need not sound alike.
Repetition remains useful when a fixed taxonomy demands parallel form; outside
that setting, it makes distinct ideas sound interchangeable.

\subsection{Cadence extends across paragraphs}

A paragraph break does not reset the rhythm. Several list-shaped sentences remain
monotonous when split into separate paragraphs, and a short heading followed by
a one-line paragraph can continue the same staccato rhythm. Human cadence is
therefore a passage-level property. Sentences pick up context from one another,
and paragraph boundaries mark changes in the reasoning rather than
opportunities to restart the same pattern.
