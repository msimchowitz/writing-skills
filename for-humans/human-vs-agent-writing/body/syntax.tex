\section{Local fluency can conceal relational errors}
\label{sec:syntax}

A phrase can sound natural while the sentence as a whole remains stiff or
describes the wrong relation. Phrase substitution alone does not resolve this
problem. The subject, verb, and modifiers have to form a coherent account of
who acts, what changes, and under which conditions.

\subsection{Question wrappers delay direct claims}

A recurring agent-like construction introduces a known relation with a
question word even though no question remains open. A noun or declarative
clause can bring the answer into view earlier.

\agentdraft

Each contribution should state what the study learned. The next section
explains how the controller filters sensor noise.

\humanrevision

Each contribution should state the outcome of the study. The controller
filters sensor noise by averaging five readings.

The first revision names the object directly, while the second places the
filtering action in the main clause and its mechanism in a modifying phrase.
The wrapper remains appropriate when it carries real uncertainty. ``We do not
know why the controller fails'' cannot be replaced with a cause until the
evidence identifies one.

\subsection{Mechanical relative clauses distort purpose}

Relative clauses can be grammatical and still force a strained relation.
Consider a sentence in which the result appears to demand its own setup:

\agentdraft

Include only the setup that is required by the result.

\humanrevision

Include only the setup needed to interpret the result.

The revision states the purpose of the setup without personifying the result.
A reader-centered version, ``Give readers only the setup they need to
interpret the result,'' shifts the emphasis again. The preferable construction
depends on the intended focus, not on preserving active or passive voice as an
end in itself.

\subsection{Agency determines the useful subject}

Active voice helps when responsibility or mechanism matters. ``The system
preserves the results automatically'' identifies an actor that disappears
from ``The results are preserved automatically.'' Likewise, ``You do not need
a configuration file'' supplies the missing subject in ``No configuration
file needed.''

Passive voice remains useful when the procedure or affected object deserves
attention, and it may be necessary when the actor is unknown. Voice choice is
therefore an information-structure decision: the sentence should focus the
object under discussion while naming a responsible actor when that
responsibility affects the claim.

\subsection{Packed catalogs hide governing relations}

A comma series can conceal the relation among ideas even when it contains only
three objects. Fixed taxonomies, procedures, and option sets are different
because readers need their complete membership. An ordinary inventory has no
such justification.

Several inventory sentences in one paragraph flatten distinctions further.
The syntax makes every item appear equally important, and a paragraph break
does not change the underlying rhythm.

\agentdraft

The report addresses hiring, onboarding, retention, scheduling, purchasing,
and reporting.

\humanrevision

The report separates staffing from daily operations. Staffing covers hiring,
onboarding, and retention, while daily operations include scheduling,
purchasing, and reporting.

The revision supplies two categories, which make later reference possible and
explain why the six terms belong in the same passage. Here the parallel lists
are informative because they instantiate a named division.

\subsection{Stacked hedges blur calibrated uncertainty}

``Could potentially possibly be argued'' contains several weak signals but no
clearer account of confidence. One well-chosen modal or condition carries more
information: ``may,'' ``likely,'' ``under these conditions,'' and ``the
available data suggest'' each locate uncertainty differently. Additional
words do not produce a finer calibration unless they add another evidential
distinction.
