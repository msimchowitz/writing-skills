\section{Terms and limits of the comparison}
\label{app:dimensions}

\subsection{Human and agent are evaluative labels}

In this article, \emph{human writing} refers to the positive standard distilled
from the writing skills: situated, precise prose whose details and rhythm
reflect a writer's judgment. It does not describe everything people write.
\emph{Agent writing} refers to recurrent defaults that appear when generated
prose lacks enough information about reader, evidence, voice, or purpose. It
does not describe everything an agent can produce.

This usage keeps the comparison useful without turning style into a detector.
A person can produce an agent-like passage by relying on formulas, and a model
can produce human-quality prose when it has strong context, evidence, and
revision. Authorship requires independent evidence such as drafts, version
history, or production records.

\subsection{Choppiness is a passage-level rhythm}

Choppiness includes staccato fragments, but it is not reducible to short
sentences. Repeated medium-length sentences can produce the same effect when
they begin alike, carry one clause each, and close with equal stress. A
paragraph break does not remove the pattern.

The relevant test is whether the rhythm reflects the argument. A short
sentence can isolate a real conclusion. A long sentence can hold a result and
its essential condition. Either becomes difficult when its form gives the
wrong idea too much weight or conceals the relation the reader needs.

\subsection{Sloppiness is semantic before it is grammatical}

The word \emph{sloppy} names imprecision that survives grammatical review. A
sentence may assign its subject an impossible action or pair a verb with the
wrong object. An abstract noun may also conceal the actor. Evidential
sloppiness has a related structure: support becomes proof, or an attribution
goes unnamed. Several hedges may still fail to identify the actual
uncertainty.

Better-usage treats these defects as failures of semantic compatibility.
Humanizer extends the same concern to rhetorical relations: generic
significance, loose catalogs, and corporate shorthand sound complete while
leaving the relevant evidence or reader need unspecified.

\subsection{Surface patterns do not prove authorship}

No single word, punctuation mark, or sentence length establishes that a
passage is agent-generated. Professional editing can explain regular grammar;
templates can explain visible structure; a field's shared vocabulary can
explain repeated terms. Quotations and examples retain the language of their
source.

The comparison becomes strongest when several patterns converge on one reading
effect. Repeated syntax, equal-weight lists, vague attribution, and a generic
ending may together show that a passage lacks hierarchy. That diagnosis
supports revision even when the author is known.
